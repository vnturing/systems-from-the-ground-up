## Representation sets the resolution
Assume IEEE 754 binary32, the common four-byte `float` format on our lab targets. A normal value has a sign, an eight-bit biased exponent, and 23 stored fraction bits. The leading binary one is implicit, giving 24 significant bits. The formula is `(-1)^s × (1 + fraction/2^23) × 2^(exponent-127)`.

Derive one value: sign zero, exponent 128, fraction `2^21` gives `1.25 × 2 = 2.5`. Do not treat the exponent bits as the exponent directly; subtract the bias. Exponent patterns at the ends encode zeros, subnormals, infinities, and NaNs under this assumed format. Goldberg's paper is the primary deeper reading for rounding and error analysis. [[float]]

## An increment can be smaller than the gap
In the interval `[2^k, 2^(k+1))`, adjacent normal binary32 numbers are spaced `2^(k-23)` apart. Around 1, the gap above is `2^-23`. At `2^24`, it is two. Adding one there lands exactly between representable numbers; round-to-nearest, ties-to-even chooses `2^24`.

The arithmetic did not “forget to add.” The exact mathematical result was rounded into the available set. On your compiler, check `FLT_RADIX`, `FLT_MANT_DIG`, and `FLT_EVAL_METHOD` from `<float.h>` before claiming an expression's intermediates have exactly this behavior. C permits implementation differences beyond the familiar desktop case. [[c]]

## Order becomes part of the algorithm
On an IEEE binary64 implementation with separate rounding after each operation, compare `(1e16 + -1e16) + 1` with `1e16 + (-1e16 + 1)`. The first yields one; the inner addition in the second rounds away the one, and the outer addition yields zero. A real-valued algebraic identity is not necessarily a floating-point program identity.

When summing many values, try pairwise summation or compensated accumulation. Compare to a higher-precision reference on inputs with mixed magnitudes and signs. A smaller measured error on one dataset is evidence for that dataset, not a universal numerical proof.

## Comparisons need a domain
A common comparison checks `abs(a-b) <= max(abs_tol, rel_tol*max(abs(a),abs(b)))`. Absolute tolerance handles values near zero; relative tolerance scales elsewhere. Choose tolerances from the problem's acceptable error and the algorithm's conditioning. NaNs need deliberate handling; infinities should not accidentally pass a subtraction-based test. Exact integer counts and protocol lengths should generally remain integers.

::: deep Cancellation and subnormals
Subtracting nearby rounded values can expose the error already present in each input. The subtraction is not automatically inaccurate in isolation; the resulting small value can have large relative error compared with the ideal inputs. Track where rounding entered.

Subnormals reduce precision gradually near zero by losing the implicit leading one. Some hardware modes flush them to zero for performance, changing the contract. Fast-math flags may also permit reassociation or assumptions that exclude NaNs. Record compiler flags alongside numeric results, and do not apply such flags as a generic performance switch.
:::

## Retrieve before looking
For binary32 in `[2^10, 2^11)`, derive the spacing. Then explain why the spacing doubles at the upper endpoint. Do the powers-of-two algebra without a calculator first.

::: solution Worked feedback
Spacing is `2^(10-23) = 2^-13 = 1/8192`. At `2^11`, the exponent increases by one while precision stays at 24 significant bits, so spacing becomes `2^-12`. The number of significant binary digits is fixed; the absolute gap is not.
:::
